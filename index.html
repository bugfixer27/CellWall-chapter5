Membrane: the structure and function of plasma membranes

OpenStax Biology 2e · Chapter 5 · Structure and function of plasma membranes

Every cell is a drop of water held in by a film of lipid and protein five to ten nanometres thick. This is how that film decides what gets in and what gets out.

Recap · 4.3–4.4

The cell, inside

§4.3 · Compartments

A cell is a city of membranes.

Membranes wall off the nucleus, the endoplasmic reticulum, the Golgi apparatus, lysosomes and mitochondria. Each compartment keeps its own chemistry.

A lysosome’s enzymes work near pH 5, far more acidic than the cytosol. Mitochondria, wrapped in two membranes, make the ATP that active transport spends in §5.3.

§4.4 · The endomembrane system

Ride one vesicle out of the ER.

Ribosomes on the rough ER build a membrane protein and thread it into the ER membrane. A transport vesicle buds off, carrying it.

The outer nuclear membrane is continuous with the rough ER. The smooth ER, without ribosomes, makes lipids.

cis → trans

Through the Golgi, out to the surface.

The vesicle fuses with the Golgi’s cis face. Sugars are trimmed and added as the protein moves to the trans face, where a secretory vesicle leaves for the plasma membrane.

Other vesicles from the trans face become lysosomes.

One membrane, passed from hand to hand: nuclear envelope, ER, Golgi, vesicles, lysosomes, plasma membrane.

Recap · 4.5–4.6

Scaffold and seams

§4.5 · The cytoskeleton

Three fibres hold the cell in shape.

Actin microfilaments (7 nm) form a cortex under the membrane. Intermediate filaments (8–10 nm) bear tension. Microtubules (25 nm) radiate from the centrosome as tracks for vesicles.

Motor proteins haul vesicles along microtubules. The nuclear lamina lining the nuclear envelope is made of intermediate filaments.

Why it matters for chapter 5

The membrane is tethered from inside.

Peripheral proteins tie the membrane to the actin cortex. In §5.4, actin pushes the membrane out into the arms that engulf a bacterium.

Actin also helps pinch off vesicles.

§4.6 · Connections between cells

Where cells meet, membranes are joined.

Tight junctions seal cells watertight. Desmosomes spot-weld them. Gap junctions are channels from cell to cell. Plant cells connect through plasmodesmata.

Desmosomes use cadherins tied to intermediate filaments; gap junctions are made of connexin proteins. Outside, integrins in the membrane grip the collagen and proteoglycans of the extracellular matrix.

5.1

Fluid mosaic

1972 · Singer & Nicolson

A mosaic that flows.

The fluid mosaic model: phospholipids, cholesterol, proteins and carbohydrates, all free to drift sideways past one another.

It is still the best description of the membrane half a century later.

More than a wall

It bends, identifies, listens.

It lets blood cells squeeze through capillaries. Its markers let cells recognise one another. Its receptors pass signals inward.

Viruses such as HIV hijack those receptors to get in, and mutations in receptor genes can break the signalling.

5–10 nm thick

The thinnest thing that matters.

A red blood cell, about 8 µm across, is roughly 1 000 times wider than the membrane around it.

This patch is 60 nm square: 1/130 of a red cell’s width.

Composition by mass

Half protein, not half lipid.

A typical human cell membrane is about 50% protein, 40% lipid and 10% carbohydrate. The recipe follows the job.

Myelin, which insulates nerve fibres, is 76% lipid. The inner mitochondrial membrane, packed with ATP-making machinery, is 76% protein. A red cell’s plasma membrane is 30% lipid.

5.1

Phospholipids

Anatomy · Fig. 5.3

Two natures in one molecule.

A hydrophilic head, a phosphate group on glycerol, and two hydrophobic tails of fatty acid. A molecule with both natures is amphiphilic.

Glycerol has three carbons: carbons 1 and 2 carry the fatty acids, carbon 3 the phosphate group, which is polar and sometimes charged.

In water

Water sorts them out.

Heads form hydrogen bonds with water; tails can’t. Here every lipid has been scattered into water.

Predict

No energy, no instructions. What do scattered phospholipids do in water?

  1. AStay scattered
  2. BGather tails in, heads out
  3. CSink to the bottom

Scroll to find out ↓

Answer · B · Fig. 5.4

Micelles, liposomes, bilayers.

They assemble on their own, burying their tails. One layer closed around its tails is a micelle. Two layers tail to tail make a bilayer, which closes into a hollow liposome.

An open bilayer edge would expose tails to water, so bilayers close up. Single-tailed lipids favour micelles; two-tailed phospholipids favour bilayers.

How the model was found

Eighty years to see it.

Found in the 1890s; lipid and protein by 1915; a protein–lipid sandwich in 1935; a “railroad track” in 1950s electron micrographs; the fluid mosaic in 1972.

The sandwich was Davson and Danielli’s. The railroad track’s two dark lines are the two layers of phospholipid heads.

    Takeaway

    1. Phospholipid: hydrophilic head, two hydrophobic tails (amphiphilic).
    2. In water they self-assemble: micelles, bilayers, liposomes.
    3. In a bilayer the heads face water and the tails hide inside.
    5.1

    Fluidity

    Cross-section · 37 °C

    A two-dimensional liquid.

    Lipids drift sideways, and one swaps places with a neighbour about ten million times a second. Heads face the water; tails fill the core.

    The sheet still holds together. It can burst if a cell takes in too much water, but it reseals around a fine needle when the needle is pulled out.

    Predict

    Cool the membrane to 4 °C. Which tails pack tightly and stiffen it?

    1. AStraight, saturated tails
    2. BKinked, unsaturated tails
    3. CBoth the same

    Scroll to find out ↓

    Answer · A · Cooling to 4 °C

    Straight tails pack; kinked tails can’t.

    Saturated tails are straight and pack tightly in the cold. A double bond kinks an unsaturated tail (glowing). Kinks keep neighbours apart, so the membrane stays fluid.

    Adaptation

    Cold-water animals change their fats.

    Fish shift the ratio of unsaturated to saturated fatty acids as the water cools. A saturated membrane would turn brittle and rupture more easily.

    Cholesterol

    A buffer against temperature.

    Cholesterol sits between phospholipids. In the cold it stops tails packing; in the heat it limits their movement. Fluid enough, and firm enough, over a wider range.

    Cholesterol also helps gather transmembrane proteins into lipid rafts.

    Takeaway

    1. The membrane is fluid: lipids drift sideways.
    2. Kinked, unsaturated tails keep it fluid in the cold.
    3. Cholesterol buffers fluidity against temperature.
    5.1

    Proteins and sugars

    Peripheral proteins

    On the surface, not in it.

    Peripheral proteins (yellow) sit on either face, attached to integral proteins or to lipid heads. They work as enzymes, anchors for the cytoskeleton, and recognition sites.

    The body recognises its own proteins and attacks the foreign proteins of pathogens.

    Integral proteins · Fig. 5.5

    Threaded through the bilayer.

    Integral proteins cross the hydrophobic core. One pass takes an α-helix of 20–25 mostly hydrophobic amino acids; complex ones cross up to 12 times. Some form channels.

    Some roll β-sheets into a barrel. The textbook also calls these “integrins”; strictly, integrins are one family of integral proteins, the ones that grip the extracellular matrix.

    Glycoproteins and glycolipids

    A sugar coat, outside only.

    Chains of 2–60 sugars on proteins and lipids form the glycocalyx. It holds water at the surface, and its pattern works like an ID card: “self” or “non-self”.

    It matters in immunity, in embryonic development and in cells sticking together to form tissues.

    Evolution connection · Fig. 5.6

    Viruses read the labels too.

    HIV binds CD4, a glycoprotein on helper T cells, to get in. Its own surface proteins mutate so fast that antibodies miss the next version: a big reason there is no HIV vaccine.

    HIV also destroys the immune cells that would fight it; hepatitis viruses find liver cells the same way. Career connection: immunologists (a PhD or MD, 2–3 years of specialist training and a board exam) work on vaccines, allergies, autoimmunity and transplants. Kizzmekia Corbett’s coronavirus research was instrumental in developing the Moderna COVID-19 vaccine.

    5.2

    Diffusion

    Outside the cellCytoplasm

    Passive transport

    If it dissolves in fat, it gets through.

    Small, non-polar molecules cross the hydrophobic core: O₂ (red), CO₂ (white), vitamins A, D, E and K. Ions and polar molecules bounce off; they will need proteins.

    Fat-soluble drugs and hormones cross too. The ions blocked here are Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻; sugars and amino acids are polar.

    Every substance for itself

    Downhill, with no energy spent.

    Diffusion carries a substance from high to low concentration: down its concentration gradient. O₂ moves in while CO₂ moves out, each on its own.

    A gradient is stored potential energy, and diffusion spends it.

    Dynamic equilibrium

    Then nothing changes, though everything moves.

    Once the two sides are even, molecules keep crossing, but equally both ways: no net movement.

    The chart on the right is counted live from the molecules on screen.

    Rate of diffusion

    Distance is why cells are small.

    O₂ crosses 10 µm in 17 ms, 1 mm in 3 min, 1 cm in 5 h. Time grows with the square of distance.

    So large cells must be flat or have their own transport systems. In filtration, pressure helps: blood pressure forces water and solutes out of the blood into the kidney’s tubules.

    5.2

    Facilitated transport

    Outside the cellCytoplasm

    Channel proteins · aquaporin

    A tunnel lined with water.

    In facilitated transport a protein gives ions and polar molecules a way through, still downhill. Aquaporins pass water single file, very fast, both ways.

    Channel proteins are transmembrane proteins with a hydrophilic pore. Aquaporins matter most in red blood cells and in the kidney’s tubules.

    Gated channels · Fig. 5.9

    Some channels wait for a signal.

    Gated channels open only when triggered. When this K⁺ channel opens, K⁺ streams out down its gradient, tens of millions of ions a second.

    Open channels are always open. Nerve and muscle cells fire and contract with gated Na⁺, K⁺ and Ca²⁺ channels.

    One at a time, by changing shape.

    A carrier binds its passenger, changes shape and releases it. Once every carrier is busy they are saturated: a steeper gradient no longer helps.

    A carrier moves 10³–10⁶ molecules a second. In diabetes the kidney “spills” glucose into the urine because its glucose carriers can’t reclaim it all. GLUT proteins carry glucose and other hexose sugars.

    5.2

    Osmosis and tonicity

    Osmosis · Fig. 5.11

    Water moves toward the solute.

    Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a membrane the solute can’t cross. Water moves from the side with less solute (more free water) to the side with more.

    It continues until the gradient is gone or the pressure of the rising column balances it. Two full glasses, one with a teaspoon of sugar and one with a quarter cup: the first holds more water.

    Tonicity · Fig. 5.12

    Hypertonic: the cell shrivels.

    Tonicity is how a solution changes a cell’s volume. With more solute outside (higher osmolarity), water leaves, and the red cell shrinks and puckers.

    The puckering is called crenation. The denser cytosol slows diffusion inside the cell.

    Predict

    Now drop a red cell into pure water. What happens?

    1. ANothing
    2. BIt shrinks
    3. CIt swells until it bursts

    Watch the cell on the right ↓

    Answer · C · Hypotonic

    Hypotonic: it swells and bursts.

    Water floods in. The membrane barely stretches, so the cell rounds into a sphere at about 1.6× its volume, then lyses, leaving an empty “ghost”.

    That point comes at about 150 mOsm/L. Past it, the gaps between lipids and proteins tear open.

    Isotonic

    Isotonic: no net change.

    With equal osmolarity inside and out, water still crosses both ways, but the volume holds. 0.9% saline is about 308 mOsm/L, close to blood.

    Osmolarity counts particles, not their size: a clear solution can out-pull a cloudy one full of cells. If a patient’s red cells burst after an “isotonic” drip, it was really hypotonic.

    One graph · Boyle–van ’t Hoff

    One curve predicts all three.

    Less solute outside means a bigger cell. A fourth cell rides the curve from 600 mOsm/L down to where it bursts, at about 150.

    V/V₀ = b + (1 − b)·300/π, with b = 0.4, the part of a red cell that water can’t swell.

    Predict

    A plant cell in pure water. Does it burst too?

    1. AYes, like the red cell
    2. BNo, it becomes firm
    3. CNo, it shrinks

    Scroll to find out ↓

    Answer · B · Fig. 5.13

    A wall changes the rules.

    The cell wall caps the curve. In pure water the membrane presses on the wall: turgor. Isotonic: flaccid. Hypertonic: the membrane pulls from the wall, plasmolysis, and the plant wilts.

    Plant cytoplasm stays slightly hypertonic, so water enters until the wall pushes back. Turgor holds up non-woody plants. Fungi and bacteria have walls too.

    Osmoregulation

    Everyone else pays to stay in balance.

    Paramecia and amoebae pump water out through contractile vacuoles. Fish spend about 5% of their energy on it. In us, the kidneys set the body’s water.

    Marine invertebrates match the sea. Freshwater fish absorb salt through their gills and make dilute urine; saltwater fish excrete salt and make concentrated urine. When blood solutes rise, osmoreceptors in the brain trigger a hormone that makes the kidneys hold water. Albumin, too large to cross membranes, holds water in the blood.

    1. Osmosis: water moves toward more solute.
    2. Hypertonic: cell shrinks. Hypotonic: it swells, and an animal cell bursts. Isotonic: no change.
    3. A plant’s wall turns swelling into turgor.

    5.3

    Active transport

    Primary active transport

    Uphill takes a pump.

    Moving a substance against its gradient costs energy. The Na⁺/K⁺ pump pays for it directly in ATP.

    This is primary active transport. Red blood cells spend most of their energy on this pump, and metabolic poisons that stop ATP production stop it too.

    Na⁺/K⁺ pump · step 1

    Three Na⁺ bind.

    Facing the cytoplasm, the pump has a high affinity for Na⁺. Three Na⁺ (gold) bind.

    Step 2

    ATP is split.

    The pump hydrolyses ATP. The phosphate group sticks to the pump; ADP leaves.

    Step 3

    It turns to face outside.

    The pump changes shape and opens outward. Its affinity for Na⁺ drops, and the three Na⁺ leave.

    Step 4

    Two K⁺ bind.

    The new shape has a high affinity for K⁺ (violet). Two K⁺ bind, and the phosphate detaches.

    Step 5

    It turns back inward.

    Without the phosphate, the pump returns to its inward-facing shape.

    Step 6

    K⁺ is released.

    Its affinity for K⁺ is now low, so two K⁺ enter the cytoplasm. Affinity for Na⁺ is high again, and the cycle restarts.

    Electrogenic

    Three out, two in: a charge.

    Each cycle moves one net positive charge out, making the inside more negative: an electrogenic pump. It does about 44 kJ/mol of work, paid from ATP’s ~50.

    Tip the membrane over

    What did the pump build? A hill.

    Let height stand for free energy. Outside the cell is at the back, the cytoplasm in front, and the membrane is the step between them.

    This is a map of energy per mole of ion, not of distance. Heights come from the concentrations and voltage in src/science.

    The electrochemical gradient

    Ions feel two pulls.

    Concentration alone makes Na⁺’s outside a hill. Now switch on the membrane potential: the negative inside (about −70 mV) pulls every positive ion in. Together: the electrochemical gradient.

    Negatively charged proteins that can’t leave the cell add to the negative interior.

    Na⁺ vs K⁺

    Na⁺ is pushed in twice. K⁺ is torn.

    Both pulls send Na⁺ inward: a steep drop. For K⁺, concentration points out but charge pulls in, so its ground is nearly level and it leaks slowly out.

    Nernst potentials at 37 °C: ENa ≈ +67 mV, EK ≈ −95 mV.

    Cotransport

    Carrier designs

    Uniporter, symporter, antiporter.

    A uniporter carries one kind of molecule; a symporter carries two the same way; an antiporter carries two opposite ways. The Na⁺/K⁺ pump is an antiporter, lifting both ions uphill.

    The same designs also work in facilitated diffusion, without ATP.

    Predict

    The glucose symporter spends no ATP. So what pays to push glucose uphill?

    1. AHeat
    2. BNa⁺ rolling downhill
    3. CNothing: it is passive

    Answer · B · Secondary active transport

    Sodium, running downhill, pays for glucose.

    The pump piled Na⁺ up outside. Through a symporter, 2 Na⁺ roll down and haul 1 glucose up with them. The pump stored the energy; this step spends it.

    This is secondary active transport. 2 Na⁺ entering release about 26 kJ/mol; moving glucose into a cell that already holds 10× more costs about 6. Amino acids enter cells the same way. This symporter couples 2 Na⁺ to each glucose, as SGLT1 does.

    Inner mitochondrial membrane

    Protons turn a motor.

    H⁺ pumped out of the matrix flows back through ATP synthase. Each H⁺ turns the rotor one subunit; one full turn makes three ATP.

    The gradient’s potential energy becomes the rotor’s kinetic energy. The ring has 8 subunits in mammals and 10 in yeast; it is drawn here with 10.

    5.4

    Bulk transport

    Phagocytosis · “cell eating”

    Wrap it, swallow it, digest it.

    Some cargo is too big to cross. In endocytosis the membrane wraps it and pinches off, which takes energy. In phagocytosis the cargo is a whole bacterium; its vesicle then fuses with a lysosome that digests it.

    Neutrophils, a kind of white blood cell, destroy invaders this way. The textbook shows a clathrin-coated region here; phagocytosis is mainly driven by actin, and clathrin is the signature of receptor-mediated endocytosis.

    Pinocytosis · potocytosis

    Sipping.

    Pinocytosis, “cell drinking”, takes in droplets of fluid and whatever is dissolved in them. Potocytosis uses caveolin to form even smaller caveolae.

    Warren Lewis described pinocytosis in 1929. Its vesicles are small and don’t fuse with lysosomes. Caveolae are rich in receptors and lipid rafts; some carry cargo right across the cell (transcytosis) or deliver it to the ER.

    Predict

    In some families the LDL receptor is missing. What happens to cholesterol in the blood?

    1. AIt falls

    Answer · B · Receptor-mediated endocytosis

    Only what was ordered.

    Receptors bind one cargo, here LDL carrying cholesterol, and gather in a pit lined with clathrin. Without the receptors LDL can’t be cleared, and cholesterol climbs to life-threatening levels.

    The disease is familial hypercholesterolaemia. Flu viruses, diphtheria toxin and cholera toxin use receptors like these to get in.

    Exocytosis

    The same thing, in reverse.

    A vesicle fuses with the membrane and opens outward, releasing its cargo. Its membrane becomes part of the plasma membrane.

    Cells secrete extracellular-matrix proteins this way, and neurons release neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft.

    All at once

    Every way across, at the same time.

    Back to the whole cell. Right now it is eating, drinking and secreting, while ions, water and gases cross by the billion.

    Unroll the membrane

    Peel off its surface and lay it flat.

    The whole plasma membrane, unrolled like the skin of a globe. Every way across it fits on this one map.

    Passive · downhill

    Passive: down the gradient, free.

    Diffusion (small, non-polar), osmosis (water), and facilitated transport through channels and carriers (ions, glucose).

    Active · uphill

    Active: against the gradient, paid for.

    Primary active transport spends ATP directly (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺). Secondary spends a gradient that a pump built (amino acids, lactose).

    Bulk · by vesicle

    Bulk: through the membrane itself.

    Phagocytosis (cells, large particles), pinocytosis and potocytosis (fluid), receptor-mediated endocytosis (specific macromolecules). All of it takes energy.

    Back to the cell

    Passive when it can, active when it must.

    Small non-polar molecules slip through the lipids. Water, ions and sugars ride proteins downhill. Uphill work is paid in ATP, directly or through a gradient. Anything too big goes by vesicle.

    A cell is whatever its membrane decides to keep.
