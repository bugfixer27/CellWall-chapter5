Membrane: the structure and function of plasma membranes

OpenStax Biology 2e · Chapter 5 · Structure and function of plasma membranes

Every cell is a drop of water held in by a film of lipid and protein five to ten nanometres thick. This is how that film decides what gets in and what gets out.

Recap · 4.3–4.4

The cell, inside

§4.3 · Compartments

A eukaryotic cell is a city of membranes.

Inside the plasma membrane, more membranes wall off the nucleus, the endoplasmic reticulum, the Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, vesicles and mitochondria. Each compartment keeps its own chemistry. A lysosome's digestive enzymes, for example, work at a pH near 5, far more acidic than the cytosol around them. Mitochondria, each wrapped in two membranes, make the ATP that active transport will spend in §5.3.

§4.4 · The endomembrane system

One membrane, passed from hand to hand.

The nuclear envelope, ER, Golgi, vesicles, lysosomes and plasma membrane work as one system. The outer nuclear membrane is continuous with the rough ER. Vesicles bud from the ER, fuse with the Golgi's cis face, and leave its trans face for lysosomes or for the plasma membrane. The bright specks travelling outward are vesicles.

Why it matters for chapter 5

Membrane proteins are built inside out.

Ribosomes on the rough ER make membrane proteins and thread them into the ER membrane. Sugars are added in the ER and modified in the Golgi. When the vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane, the face that pointed into the ER now points out of the cell. That is why glycoproteins and glycolipids always carry their sugars on the outside.

Recap · 4.5–4.6

Scaffold and seams

§4.5 · The cytoskeleton

Three kinds of fibre hold the cell in shape.

Microfilaments of actin (about 7 nm) form a dense cortex just under the plasma membrane. Intermediate filaments (8–10 nm) bear tension and anchor the nucleus; the lamina lining the nuclear envelope is made of them. Microtubules (about 25 nm) radiate from the centrosome, and motor proteins haul vesicles along them.

Why it matters for chapter 5

The membrane is tethered from inside.

Peripheral proteins on the membrane’s cytoplasmic face tie it to cytoskeletal fibres. Actin also does the heavy work of bulk transport in §5.4: it pushes the membrane out into the arms that engulf a bacterium, and helps pinch off vesicles.

§4.6 · Connections between cells

Where cells meet, membranes are joined.

Tight junctions seal neighbouring animal cells so fluid can’t leak between them. Desmosomes spot-weld cells together, with cadherins tied to intermediate filaments. Gap junctions are channels of connexin proteins that let ions and small molecules pass directly between cells. Plant cells connect through their walls by plasmodesmata. Outside, integrins in the membrane grip the collagen and proteoglycans of the extracellular matrix.

5.1

Fluid mosaic

1972 · Singer & Nicolson

A mosaic that flows.

The fluid mosaic model describes the plasma membrane as a mosaic of phospholipids, cholesterol, proteins and carbohydrates that are free to move sideways past one another. It is still the best description of the membrane half a century later.

More than a wall

It bends, it identifies, it listens.

The membrane lets red and white blood cells change shape to squeeze through capillaries. It carries the markers that let cells recognise one another. Its receptors pick up signals from outside and pass them in. Viruses such as HIV hijack those receptors to get in, and mutations in receptor genes can break the signalling.

5–10 nm thick

The thinnest thing that matters.

The membrane is 5 to 10 nm thick. A red blood cell, about 8 µm across, is roughly 1 000 times wider than the membrane around it. This patch is 60 nm square: 1/130 of a red cell’s width.

Composition by mass

Half protein, not half lipid.

In a typical human cell membrane, proteins make up about 50% of the mass, lipids about 40% and carbohydrates the remaining 10%. The recipe follows the job. Myelin, which insulates nerve fibres, is 76% lipid. The inner mitochondrial membrane, packed with the machinery that makes ATP, is 76% protein. A red cell’s plasma membrane is 30% lipid.

5.1

Phospholipids

Anatomy · Fig. 5.3

Two natures in one molecule.

A phospholipid is a three-carbon glycerol backbone. Carbons 1 and 2 each carry a fatty acid; carbon 3 carries a phosphate-containing group. The phosphate head is polar, sometimes charged, and hydrophilic. The fatty-acid tails are non-polar and hydrophobic. A molecule with both natures is amphiphilic.

In water

Water sorts them out.

Heads form hydrogen bonds with water; tails can’t. Scattered in water, phospholipids assemble on their own, with no energy spent and no instructions, burying their tails away from water and turning their heads out toward it.

Fig. 5.4

Micelles, liposomes, bilayers.

A single layer closed around its tails is a micelle. Two layers tail to tail make a bilayer, which closes into a hollow sphere, a liposome, because an open edge would expose tails to water. Lipids with one tail favour micelles; two-tailed phospholipids favour bilayers.

How the model was found

Eighty years to see it.

The membrane was identified in the 1890s, and by 1915 it was known to be lipid and protein. In 1935 Davson and Danielli proposed a sandwich of lipid between two layers of protein. Electron micrographs in the 1950s showed a “railroad track”, two dark lines that turned out to be the two layers of phospholipid heads. In 1972 Singer and Nicolson proposed the fluid mosaic.

    5.1

    Fluidity

    Cross-section · 37 °C

    A two-dimensional liquid.

    Phospholipids and many proteins drift sideways, and a lipid swaps places with a neighbour about ten million times a second. The sheet still holds together. It is fairly rigid and can burst if a cell takes in too much water, but it reseals around a fine needle when the needle is pulled out.

    Cooling to 4 °C

    Straight tails pack; kinked tails can’t.

    A saturated fatty acid has no double bonds, so its tail is straight, and straight tails pack tightly as the temperature falls: the membrane gets denser and stiffer. A double bond in an unsaturated tail puts a kink in it, highlighted here. The kinks keep the neighbours apart, so the membrane stays fluid in the cold.

    Adaptation

    Cold-water animals change their fats.

    Many organisms, fish among them, change the ratio of unsaturated to saturated fatty acids in their membranes as the temperature changes. A membrane rich in saturated fats would pack tight and become brittle in cold water, and more likely to rupture.

    Cholesterol

    A buffer against temperature.

    Cholesterol’s four fused rings sit alongside the phospholipids. In the cold it stops the tails from packing together; in the heat it limits how much they move. The membrane stays fluid enough, and firm enough, over a wider range of temperatures. Cholesterol also helps gather transmembrane proteins into lipid rafts.

    5.1

    Proteins and sugars

    Peripheral proteins

    On the surface, not in it.

    Peripheral proteins (yellow) sit on the inner or outer face, attached to integral proteins or to phospholipid heads. They work as enzymes, as attachment points for the cytoskeleton, and as cell-specific recognition sites. The body recognises its own proteins and attacks the foreign proteins of pathogens.

    Integral proteins · Fig. 5.5

    Threaded through the bilayer.

    Integral proteins cross the hydrophobic core. A single-pass protein needs one α-helix of 20–25 mostly hydrophobic amino acids. Complex ones cross up to 12 times, or roll β-sheets into a barrel. Their hydrophobic faces lie against the tails; their hydrophilic parts reach into the cytoplasm and the extracellular fluid. Some form channels with a pore down the middle. The textbook also calls these “integrins”. Strictly, integrins are one family of integral proteins: the ones that grip the extracellular matrix.

    Glycoproteins and glycolipids

    A sugar coat, outside only.

    Chains of 2 to 60 sugars, straight or branched, are attached to proteins (glycoproteins) and lipids (glycolipids) on the outer face. Together they form the glycocalyx. It is strongly hydrophilic and holds a layer of water at the cell surface. Its pattern works like an ID card: it is how the immune system tells “self” from “non-self”, and it matters in embryonic development and in cells sticking together to form tissues.

    Evolution connection · Fig. 5.6

    Viruses read the labels too.

    HIV binds CD4, a glycoprotein on helper T cells, to get in. Its own surface proteins mutate so quickly that antibodies against one version miss the next, which is a big part of why there is still no HIV vaccine. HIV also destroys the very immune cells that would fight it. Hepatitis viruses find liver cells the same way. Career connection: immunologists (a PhD or MD, then 2–3 years of specialist training and a board exam) work on vaccines, allergies, autoimmunity and transplants. Kizzmekia Corbett’s coronavirus research was instrumental in developing the Moderna COVID-19 vaccine.

    5.2

    Diffusion

    Outside the cellCytoplasm

    Passive transport

    If it dissolves in fat, it gets through.

    The hydrophobic core lets small, non-polar molecules cross easily: O₂ (red), CO₂ (white), the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K, and fat-soluble drugs and hormones. Ions such as Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻, and polar molecules such as sugars and amino acids, bounce off. They will need proteins.

    Every substance for itself

    Downhill, with no energy spent.

    Diffusion carries a substance from where it is concentrated to where it is scarce. Here O₂ moves into the cell while CO₂ moves out, each down its own concentration gradient, independently. A gradient is stored potential energy, and diffusion spends it.

    Dynamic equilibrium

    Then nothing changes, though everything moves.

    Once the two sides are even, molecules keep crossing, but equally in both directions, so there is no net movement. The chart on the right is counted live from the molecules on screen.

    Rate of diffusion

    Distance is why cells are small.

    O₂ diffuses across a 10 µm cell in about 17 ms, across 1 mm in 3 min, and across 1 cm in 5 h. Time grows with the square of distance, so large cells must be flat or have their own transport systems. Pressure can speed diffusion along: in filtration, blood pressure forces water and solutes out of the blood into the kidney’s tubules.

    5.2

    Facilitated transport

    Outside the cellCytoplasm

    Channel proteins · aquaporin

    A tunnel lined with water.

    Ions and polar molecules can still move downhill if a protein gives them a way through. Channel proteins are transmembrane proteins with a hydrophilic pore. Aquaporins (blue) let water through very fast, one molecule behind another, both ways at once. They matter most in red blood cells and in the kidney’s tubules.

    Gated channels · Fig. 5.9

    Some channels wait for a signal.

    Open channels are always open; gated channels open only when an ion binds or some other trigger acts. When this K⁺ channel (pink) opens, K⁺ streams out down its gradient. Nerve and muscle cells fire and contract with gated Na⁺, K⁺ and Ca²⁺ channels, and a single channel can pass tens of millions of ions a second.

    Carrier proteins · Fig. 5.10

    One at a time, by changing shape.

    A carrier (violet) binds its passenger, changes shape, and releases it on the other side, a thousand to a million molecules a second. A membrane has only so many carriers, and once they are all busy they are saturated: a steeper gradient no longer speeds things up. That is why, in diabetes, the kidney “spills” glucose into the urine: its glucose carriers can’t reclaim it all. GLUT proteins carry glucose and other hexose sugars.

    5.2

    Osmosis and tonicity

    Osmosis · Fig. 5.11

    Water moves toward the solute.

    Osmosis is the diffusion of free water across a membrane that the solute can’t cross. Water moves from the side with more free water (less solute) to the side with less, until the gradient is gone or the pressure of the rising column balances it. Two full glasses, one with a teaspoon of sugar and one with a quarter cup: the first holds more water.

    Tonicity · Fig. 5.12

    Hypertonic: the cell shrivels.

    Tonicity is how a solution changes a cell’s volume. When the outside has the higher osmolarity (total solute concentration), water leaves. A red cell shrinks and puckers into spikes (crenation). Its cytosol gets denser and slows diffusion inside it.

    Hypotonic

    Hypotonic: the cell swells and bursts.

    When the outside has the lower osmolarity, water floods in. A red cell’s membrane barely stretches, so the most it can do is round up into a sphere at about 1.6× its volume. That point comes at about 150 mOsm/L. Past it, the gaps between lipids and proteins tear open, and the cell lyses, leaving an empty “ghost”.

    Isotonic

    Isotonic: no net change.

    With equal osmolarity inside and out, water still moves both ways, but the volume holds. Osmolarity counts particles, not their size: a clear solution can out-pull a cloudy one full of cells. 0.9% saline is about 308 mOsm/L, close to blood. So if a patient’s red cells burst after an “isotonic” drip, the saline was not isotonic. It was hypotonic.

    Fig. 5.13 · Plants, fungi, bacteria

    A wall changes the rules.

    A cell wall keeps plant cells from bursting. Their cytoplasm stays slightly hypertonic, so water enters until the membrane presses against the wall. This is turgor (right), and it holds up non-woody plants. In an isotonic solution the cell is flaccid (centre). In a hypertonic one the membrane pulls away from the wall. This is plasmolysis (left), and the plant wilts.

    Osmoregulation

    Everyone else pays to stay in balance.

    Paramecia and amoebae pump water out through contractile vacuoles. Marine invertebrates simply match the sea. Fish spend about 5% of their energy on it: freshwater fish absorb salt through their gills and make dilute urine, while saltwater fish excrete salt and make concentrated urine. In us, kidneys set the body’s water. Osmoreceptors in the brain trigger a hormone that makes the kidneys hold water when blood solutes rise. Albumin, too large to cross membranes, holds water in the blood.

    5.3

    Active transport

    Outside the cellCytoplasm

    The electrochemical gradient

    Ions feel two pulls.

    An ion responds to its concentration gradient and to the voltage across the membrane. Together they make its electrochemical gradient. The inside of a living cell is negative (about −70 mV in a neuron at rest). It holds more K⁺ (violet) and less Na⁺ (gold) than the fluid outside. Negatively charged proteins that can’t leave add to the charge.

    Na⁺ vs K⁺

    Na⁺ is pushed in twice. K⁺ is torn.

    For Na⁺, both its concentration gradient and the negative interior point inward. For K⁺, its concentration gradient points out, but the charge pulls it in. Nernst potentials at 37 °C: ENa ≈ +67 mV, EK ≈ −95 mV.

    Na⁺/K⁺ pump · step 1

    Three Na⁺ bind.

    Moving ions against their gradient costs energy, paid here directly in ATP. Primary active transport. Facing the inside, the pump has a high affinity for Na⁺, and three Na⁺ bind.

    Step 2

    ATP is split.

    The pump hydrolyses ATP. A low-energy phosphate group attaches to the pump, and ADP leaves.

    Step 3

    It turns to face outside.

    The pump changes shape and opens to the outside. Its affinity for Na⁺ drops, and the three Na⁺ leave.

    Step 4

    Two K⁺ bind.

    The new shape has a high affinity for K⁺. Two K⁺ bind, and the phosphate group detaches.

    Step 5

    It turns back to face inside.

    With the phosphate gone and K⁺ bound, the pump returns to its inward-facing shape.

    Step 6

    K⁺ is released. Again.

    Now its affinity for K⁺ is low, and the two K⁺ enter the cytoplasm. The affinity for Na⁺ is high again, and the cycle restarts.

    Electrogenic

    Three out, two in: a charge.

    Each cycle moves one net positive charge out, making the inside slightly more negative. It is an electrogenic pump. One cycle does about 44 kJ of work per mole, paid from ATP’s ~50. Red blood cells spend most of their energy on it, and metabolic poisons that stop ATP production stop it too.

    5.3

    Cotransport

    Outside the cellCytoplasm

    Carrier designs

    Uniporter, symporter, antiporter.

    A uniporter carries one kind of ion or molecule. A symporter carries two different ones in the same direction, and an antiporter carries them in opposite directions. The Na⁺/K⁺ pump is an antiporter. The same designs also work in facilitated diffusion, without ATP.

    Secondary active transport

    Sodium, running downhill, pays for glucose.

    The pump piles Na⁺ up outside. When Na⁺ flows back in through a symporter (orange), down its electrochemical gradient, it brings glucose with it, against glucose’s own gradient. No ATP is spent at this step. The energy was stored in the Na⁺ gradient by primary active transport. Amino acids enter cells the same way. This symporter couples 2 Na⁺ to each glucose, as SGLT1 does.

    Inner mitochondrial membrane

    Protons turn a motor.

    Cells also store energy as a gradient of H⁺. In mitochondria, H⁺ pumped out of the matrix flows back through ATP synthase, and its potential energy becomes the kinetic energy of a spinning rotor. Each H⁺ advances the ring one subunit. One full turn joins ADP and phosphate into three ATP. The ring has 8 subunits in mammals and 10 in yeast; it is drawn here with 10.

    5.4

    Bulk transport

    Phagocytosis · “cell eating”

    Wrap it, swallow it, digest it.

    Some cargo is simply too big to cross. In endocytosis the membrane surrounds it and pinches off as a vesicle, and this takes energy. In phagocytosis the cargo is a whole bacterium. Neutrophils, a kind of white blood cell, destroy invaders this way. The vesicle then fuses with a lysosome (red), whose enzymes digest the contents. The textbook shows a clathrin-coated region here. Phagocytosis is mainly driven by actin; clathrin is the signature of receptor-mediated endocytosis, below.

    Pinocytosis · potocytosis

    Sipping.

    Pinocytosis (“cell drinking”, described by Warren Lewis in 1929) takes in droplets of extracellular fluid and the molecules dissolved in them. Its vesicles are much smaller and don’t fuse with lysosomes. Potocytosis uses caveolin, not clathrin, to form even smaller flask-shaped caveolae, rich in receptors and lipid rafts. Some carry cargo right across the cell (transcytosis) or deliver it to the ER.

    Receptor-mediated endocytosis

    Only what was ordered.

    Receptors bind a specific cargo, here LDL particles (gold) that carry cholesterol, and cluster in a pit coated with clathrin on its inner face. In familial hypercholesterolaemia the LDL receptors are defective or missing, so LDL can’t be cleared from the blood and cholesterol climbs to life-threatening levels. Flu viruses, diphtheria toxin and cholera toxin use receptors like these to get in.

    Exocytosis

    The same thing, in reverse.

    A vesicle fuses with the inner face of the plasma membrane and opens to the outside, releasing what it carries. Cells secrete extracellular-matrix proteins this way, and neurons release neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft. The vesicle’s membrane becomes part of the plasma membrane.

    Summary

    Every way across.

    Everything in chapter 5 fits in one table: two kinds of passive transport through the lipids and proteins, two kinds of active transport through proteins, and three ways through the membrane itself.

    Back to the cell

    Passive when it can, active when it must.

    Small non-polar molecules slip through the lipids. Water, ions and sugars ride channels and carriers downhill. Uphill work is paid for in ATP, directly or through a stored gradient. Anything too big goes by vesicle. All of it happens in the glowing cyan film around this cell.

    A cell is whatever its membrane decides to keep.
